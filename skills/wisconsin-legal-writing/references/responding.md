# When the other side acts

The rest of this skill assumes you are producing a document because you decided to.
Half of litigation is the other half: clocks that start when the opposing party
serves something, and that run whether or not you notice. A collection firm's
playbook is built on them.

The response periods in §§ 1-3 were checked against official chs. 801, 802, and
804 on 2026-09-20 (publication currency September 4, 2026). Case propositions and
local-rule summaries still require their own checks. **Compute a live deadline
from the current source, the actual service record, and any controlling order.**

---

## 1. Requests for admission are the dangerous one

**Wis. Stat. § 804.11(1)(b): the matter is admitted unless a written answer or
objection is served within the applicable response period.** Ordinarily that is
30 days after service of the request, but, unless the court shortens the time, a
defendant need not respond before **45 days after service of the summons and
complaint**. That protection is not limited to requests delivered in the same
packet as the summons. Apply the counting/service rules and any court order.

**Deemed admission is self-executing when the applicable deadline expires.**
No motion is needed to create the admission. Under § 804.11(2) it is conclusively
established unless the court permits withdrawal or amendment. Do not label
calendar day 31 or 46 as the trigger without checking counting rules, service
add-ons, and an altered response period.

**This is how consumer collection cases against unrepresented defendants end:**

1. Plaintiff serves requests for admission **with** the summons and complaint.
2. Defendant answers the complaint, feels he has responded, and does not answer the
   requests.
3. The properly computed response deadline passes. The particular matters actually
   requested may be deemed admitted.
4. Plaintiff moves for summary judgment on the admissions alone. *Bank of Two Rivers
   v. Zimmer*, 112 Wis. 2d 624, 334 N.W.2d 230 (1983), holds that is enough even
   where the admissions dispose of the whole case.
5. Defendant rests on bare pleading denials. Under § 802.08(3), summary judgment
   may follow **if appropriate**; the movant must still satisfy the governing rule.

The admission does not require a fault finding. Judgment itself requires judicial
application of the summary-judgment standard; it is not automatic.

**If time is short, answer the admissions first.** Missing an interrogatory deadline
exposes you to a motion; missing an RFA deadline can establish decisive facts.

**"I don't know" is not an answer.** § 804.11(1)(b) does not let a party give lack of
information as a reason to refuse to admit or deny "unless the party states that he
or she had made reasonable inquiry and that the information known or readily
obtainable by the party is insufficient" to admit or deny. In a debt-buyer case, "I
have no idea whether this plaintiff owns the debt" fails; the reasonable-inquiry
recitation is required.

**An objection stops the clock but is not the end.** The statute says "answer **or**
objection," so a timely objection stating its reasons prevents automatic admission.
But under § 804.11(1)(c) the requesting party can move to test the sufficiency of the
answers or objections, and that motion routes expenses through § 804.12(1)(c). Object
where warranted; answer everything you can.

### If the deadline is already blown

Move under **§ 804.11(2)** immediately, and file it before or with the summary
judgment response.

> "The court may permit withdrawal or amendment when the presentation of the merits
> of the action will be subserved thereby and the party who obtained the admission
> fails to satisfy the court that withdrawal or amendment will prejudice the party
> in maintaining the action or defense on the merits."

Two features make this better than it looks:

- **The prejudice burden is on the party who obtained the admission**, not on you.
- **Excusable neglect is reportedly not required**, unlike the § 801.15(2)(a)
  standard that governs a late answer. `[UNVERIFIED]` This comes from an annotation
  to *Luckett v. Bodner*, 2009 WI 68, 318 Wis. 2d 423, 769 N.W.2d 504. **Read
  *Luckett* before relying on it**; it is the whole basis of the motion.

Relief is still discretionary even when both statutory conditions are met. *Mucek v.
Nationwide Communications, Inc.*, 2002 WI App 60, 252 Wis. 2d 426, 643 N.W.2d 98. A
history of discovery non-compliance weighs against you, so file promptly and come
with the answers already prepared.

Note also *Schmid v. Olsen*, 111 Wis. 2d 228, 330 N.W.2d 547 (1983): denying an
allegation in your answer does **not** relieve you of having to deny it again in
response to an RFA.

## 2. The other response clocks

| Event | Period | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Answer a complaint (ordinary civil) | **20 days** from service | § 802.06(1), § 801.09(2) |
| Answer/reply when an insurer is a defendant or a cause of action in the specified pleadings sounds in tort; state and listed state actors also have a 45-day period | 45 days; check the exact category | § 802.06(1)(a) |
| Answer after service by publication | 40 days from the date in the summons | § 801.09(2) |
| **Responsive pleading after a § 802.06(2) motion is denied** | **10 days** from notice | § 802.06(1) |
| Reply to a counterclaim | Ordinarily 20 days from service of the answer; check the 45-day categories above | § 802.06(1)(a) |
| Requests for admission | Ordinarily 30 days after request service, but defendant's 45-day protection runs from service of summons and complaint unless shortened | § 804.11(1)(b) |
| Interrogatories | Ordinarily 30 days after request service; defendant may use 45 days after service of summons and complaint; court may alter time | § 804.08(1)(b) |
| Requests for production | Ordinarily 30 days after request service; defendant's 45-day period runs from service of summons and complaint; court may alter time | § 804.09(2)(b)1. |
| **Opposing affidavits on summary judgment** | **At least 5 days before the hearing**, unless earlier times are specified in the scheduling order | § 802.08(2) |

Two of these catch people out. **The 10-day tail** after a motion to dismiss is
denied is short and easy to miss; the court may set another period. A **debt-buyer
contract claim alone does not invoke the tort-based 45-day period**. Check all
pleadings, defendant categories, service method, and any order before choosing a
period. A qualifying § 802.06 motion can also stay discovery under (1)(b); check
that provision rather than assuming all discovery clocks continue unchanged.

**Small claims has no statutory written-answer deadline**: the controlling event is
the return date, §§ 799.05, 799.20, 799.22. But many counties impose a written-answer
requirement by local rule under § 799.06(1), and **La Crosse Rule 704 requires a
written answer by 10:00 a.m. on the return date** (4:30 p.m. the Monday after, in
evictions). Check the local rule; the statute alone will mislead you.

## 3. At summary judgment, bare pleading denials are insufficient

**§ 802.08(3):**

> "When a motion for summary judgment is made and supported as provided in this
> section, an adverse party may not rest upon the mere allegations or denials of the
> pleadings but the adverse party's response, by affidavits or as otherwise provided
> in this section, must set forth specific facts showing that there is a genuine
> issue for trial."

A bare denial, even in a verified pleading, does not itself establish a factual
dispute. Use admissible evidentiary facts through the materials allowed by
§ 802.08(2)-(3), which include depositions, interrogatory answers, admissions, and
affidavits. A compliant § 887.015 declaration can supply personal-knowledge facts;
it is not the only permissible response. Attach and serve referenced papers **if
not already of record**, as § 802.08(3) provides. See `circuit-court.md` § 3.

Check the scheduling order and the § 801.15 counting rules before working backward
from a hearing. If essential evidence is unavailable, consider the supported
request contemplated by § 802.08(4); do not simply wait for an adjournment.

Sources: [ch. 801](https://docs.legis.wisconsin.gov/statutes/statutes/801.pdf),
[ch. 802](https://docs.legis.wisconsin.gov/statutes/statutes/802.pdf),
[ch. 804](https://docs.legis.wisconsin.gov/statutes/statutes/804.pdf).

## 4. Costs run both ways, and the arithmetic is against a pro se litigant

The skill's discovery material presents § 804.12(1)(c) as a weapon. Read the whole
subsection. It has two branches.

> "**If the motion is denied**, the court **shall**, after opportunity for hearing,
> require the moving party or the attorney advising the motion or both of them to pay
> to the party or deponent who opposed the motion the reasonable expenses incurred in
> opposing the motion, including attorney fees, unless the court finds that the
> making of the motion was substantially justified or that other circumstances make
> an award of expenses unjust."

Four things about that, in order of how much they should change behaviour:

1. **"shall."** The award is mandatory once the motion is denied and no exception
   applies.
2. **"substantially justified" is yours to establish**, not the opponent's to
   disprove. After a denial the default is that you owe fees.
3. **A pro se movant is the moving party.** There is no attorney to absorb it.
4. **The exposure differs.** An unrepresented movant ordinarily has no attorney
   fees for personal time but may incur other reasonable expenses. A represented
   opponent may claim attorney fees if the statutory conditions are met. Keep the
   substantial-justification and unjust-award exceptions in the analysis.

**So do not file a motion to compel casually.** Before filing, be able to point to a
specific request, a specific inadequate response quoted verbatim, and a documented
conferral attempt. That is what "substantially justified" looks like on the record,
and it is why `practice-areas.md` § 5 insists on the conferral paragraph even though
no rule requires one.

A denied **protective order** motion carries the same exposure through
§ 804.01(3)(b). Section 804.12(4) also authorizes expenses for specified failures
without a prior order compelling discovery, subject to its substantial-
justification and unjust-award exceptions.

## 5. Sanctions reach a self-represented party, and one shield does not

**§ 802.05(2) applies to "an attorney or unrepresented party" by its own terms.**
There is no pro se carve-out. The certification that legal contentions are warranted
and factual contentions have evidentiary support is yours every time you sign.
Subsections (1) to (3) of § 802.05 "do not apply to disclosures and discovery
requests, responses, objections, and motions that are subject to ss. 804.01 to
804.12." § 802.05(5). Discovery papers answer to those sections instead, including
the § 804.12 provisions in section 4 above.

**The asymmetry worth knowing:** § 802.05(3)(b) provides that monetary sanctions may
not be awarded against a **represented** party for a violation of sub. (2)(b), the
legally-frivolous-contention branch, on the theory that the law is the lawyer's
responsibility. A self-represented litigant is both the party and the person making
the contentions, so that shield is structurally unavailable. **Proceeding pro se
removes a protection a represented party has.**

Counterweight: the sanction is capped at "what is sufficient to deter," and
nonmonetary directives are expressly authorized.

**§ 895.044** (frivolous claims) also reaches "a party," not just attorneys, and
authorizes **actual reasonable attorney fees**, which are uncapped, unlike the fixed
§ 814.04 schedule of $100 to $500. Three features cut in your favour: the standard is
**clear and convincing evidence**; a **21-day withdraw-or-correct period** (or another
period the court prescribes) bears on the cost award, § 895.044(2)(a)-(b); and
**§ 895.044(3) requires that a motion be served on an unrepresented party by personal
service or registered mail only.** A § 895.044 motion served by eFiling notice or
ordinary mail is defectively served, and that is a threshold objection.

**Do not cite § 814.025.** It was repealed effective July 1, 2005 by Supreme Court
Order 03-06. Citing it signals stale research. The successor is § 895.044.

**Two statutes, two different 21-day periods.** A motion under § 802.05(3)(a)1. is
served but "shall not be filed with or presented to the court unless, within 21 days
after service of the motion or such other period as the court may prescribe," the
challenged paper, claim, defense, contention, allegation, or denial is not withdrawn
or appropriately corrected. Section 895.044(2) has no such filing bar: the motion may
be "made at any time during the proceeding or upon judgment." If the court finds by
clear and convincing evidence that § 895.044(1)(a) or (b) applies, a withdrawal or
appropriate correction within 21 days after service of the motion, or within another
period the court prescribes, makes the cost award discretionary, § 895.044(2)(a); if
the withdrawal or correction is not timely made, the award is mandatory,
§ 895.044(2)(b). A timely correction under § 895.044 does not guarantee that no
costs are awarded. Never ignore either kind of motion. Read what it says the problem
is, and if it is right, fix it inside the period.

## 6. You are held to the same rules

*Waushara County v. Graf*, 166 Wis. 2d 442, 452, 480 N.W.2d 16 (1992):

> "Pro se appellants must satisfy all procedural requirements, unless those
> requirements are waived by the court. They are bound by the same rules that apply
> to attorneys on appeal."

> "Neither a trial court nor a reviewing court has a duty to walk pro se litigants
> through the procedural requirements or to point them to the proper substantive
> law."

*Graf* confined the more lenient treatment in earlier cases to pro se **prisoners**,
so a non-incarcerated civil litigant gets no allowance. Its home ground is appellate
procedure, but courts apply the principle in circuit court.

This is the reason the clocks in this file matter more than anything else in the
skill. No one is going to warn you.

## 7. What to ask before drafting anything

Because these clocks run silently, establish posture before writing a word:

- What has the other side served, and **on what date were you served**?
- Were requests for admission among them? Has that period run?
- Is there a scheduling order, and what does it set?
- Is a hearing scheduled? On what?
- What is the next deadline in this case, and is it already past?
- Has conferral happened, and what exactly was said?

If the user cannot answer the RFA question, ask them to check before anything else.
