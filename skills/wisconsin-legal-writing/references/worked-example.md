# Worked example: the Doe sever motion

`State v. Doe`, La Crosse County Case No. [CASE NO.]. Defendant's
Motion to Sever Counts 1-4 and for Relief from Prejudicial Joinder, filed by defense
counsel.

**This worked example is fictionalized.** The party, attorney, case number, agency
case number, and every date below are invented; the structure and the drafting moves
are modeled on a real, well-built filing. The
templates in `assets/` teach shape; this file teaches why the shape works. Read it
before drafting anything substantial, and steal the moves.

## The skeleton

```
CAPTION (two-column table)
DEFENDANT'S MOTION TO SEVER COUNTS 1-4 AND FOR RELIEF FROM PREJUDICIAL JOINDER

MOTION                    <- what is being asked for
I.   INTRODUCTION         <- why, in one paragraph
II.  THE CHARGES AND THE FACE OF THE COMPLAINT   <- the facts that decide it
III. ARGUMENT
     A. misjoinder (a question of law)
        1. not "same or similar character"
        2. not "connected together" / common scheme
        3. no factual narrative supporting probable cause
     B. even if joinder were proper, § 971.12(3) requires severance
        1. cross-admissibility, and there is none
        2. every Bettinger danger is acute here
        3. no instruction could cure it; no economy on the other side
        4. because misjoined, prejudice is presumed
IV.  CONCLUSION
```

Note what is **not** there: no separate "Legal Standard" section, no statement of
facts about the client's background, no procedural history beyond one sentence. Every
section earns its place.

## 1. The motion states relief before it argues anything

> The defendant, John A. Doe, by his attorney, Alex R. Attorney, respectfully
> moves the Court, pursuant to Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1) and (3) and Wis. Stat.
> § 971.31, for an order:
>
> 1. Severing Counts 1 through 4 ... and directing that Counts 1 through 4 proceed
>    to a separate preliminary examination and separate trial;
> 2. In addition or in the alternative, finding that the Amended Criminal Complaint
>    sets forth no facts establishing probable cause as to Counts 1 through 4 ... and
>    dismissing Counts 1 through 4 without prejudice; and
> 3. Granting whatever other relief justice requires. Wis. Stat. § 971.12(3).

**Why it works.** A judge knows in fifteen seconds exactly what will be signed. The
relief is enumerated, and each item is something a court can order without
interpretation. The parentheticals identify the counts by their content, so the
reader does not have to hold count numbers in memory. Item 2 is pleaded "in addition
or in the alternative," which preserves both without making them compete. Item 3 is
a tag, not a request, and it carries its own citation.

**The move to steal:** enumerate relief in the motion, and make each item an order.

## 2. Timeliness is disposed of in one paragraph, up front

> This motion can be decided without a trial of the general issue. Wis. Stat.
> § 971.31(1). It is also timely. No preliminary examination has been held, Mr.
> Doe has not been arraigned on an information, see Wis. Stat. § 971.31(5)(a),
> (5)(c), and undersigned counsel was appointed on [DATE].

**Why it works.** Procedural objections get raised and killed before the argument
starts, so nothing interrupts the merits later. Each fact establishing timeliness is
tied to the subsection it satisfies. The appointment date pre-empts a delay
objection without ever naming it.

**The move to steal:** when there is a foreseeable procedural objection, answer it in
the motion, in three sentences, and never mention it again.

## 3. The introduction is one paragraph and it is the whole case

> The Amended Criminal Complaint filed on [DATE], joins two prosecutions
> that have nothing to do with each other.

Then, in the same paragraph: what Counts 1-4 allege, what Counts 5-13 allege, and:

> The two sets of charges share no date, no location, and no complainant. The
> complaint supplies detailed sworn narratives supporting the domestic counts; for
> Counts 1-4 it supplies only the charging language itself, with no factual narrative
> behind it.

**Why it works.** First sentence is the thesis in fourteen words. The middle is
concrete: dates, streets, substances, names. The close is a rhythmic triple ("no
date, no location, no complainant") that a judge will remember and may well repeat
back in a decision. The last sentence sets up the argument that the complaint is
defective, which is Section III.A.3, without arguing it yet.

**The move to steal:** write the introduction last, and write it so that a reader who
stops there already knows what you want and roughly why.

## 4. The fact section is organized by argument, not chronology

Section II opens by charting the thirteen counts in a table, then:

> Three features of the charging document frame this motion. **First**, the two
> groups of counts are different in kind. ... **Second**, the groups are separated by
> several weeks and by geography. ... **Third, and most telling**, the sworn FACTUAL
> BASIS section of the complaint consists of three law-enforcement narratives
> concerning the domestic incidents ... The complaint contains no corresponding
> narrative for the June 1 drug allegations.

**Why it works.** The three features map onto the three things the argument needs.
Nothing else about the case appears. "Three features ... First / Second / Third"
gives the reader a structure to hold. "Third, and most telling" tells the court which
fact to care about most, which is argument doing work inside a fact section without
sounding like argument.

The last detail in the section is the sharpest: agency case 00-00-00000 "appears
once, in the caption, and nowhere else." That is a fact, stated flatly, that wins the
motion.

**The move to steal:** decide the argument first, then write only the facts it needs,
in the order it needs them. Find the one detail that makes the point without
commentary and put it last.

## 5. Point headings are propositions, and they are ordered

> **A.** Counts 1-4 are misjoined: on the face of the Amended Criminal Complaint they
> satisfy none of the statutory bases for joinder under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1).
>
> **B.** Even if joinder were technically proper, § 971.12(3) requires severance
> because a single trial would cause substantial prejudice that no instruction could
> cure.

**Why it works.** Each heading is a sentence the court could lift into a decision.
Each carries its statutory hook. "Even if" tells the reader exactly how B relates to
A, so the court knows B is a fallback and does not have to reach it.

The subheadings under A track the statutory categories in § 971.12(1) **in the order
the statute lists them**. A court checking the statute against the brief finds them
in the same sequence.

**The move to steal:** if the governing rule is a list, make your subheadings that
list, in that order.

## 6. Factor tests get walked, not asserted

Section III.A.2 quotes the seven *Salinas* factors, then:

> Measured against the four corners of the complaint, every factor cuts against
> joinder. The two groups of charges are not closely related; they involve different
> statutes, different dates, different places, and different alleged victims. No
> common fact of substantial importance ties them together. ... Neither charge is
> alleged to have grown out of the investigation of the other. There is no shared
> manner, scheme, or plan, and there is no allegation that one crime was committed to
> escape punishment for the other.

Then it does something better than walking the factors:

> That last connection is the paradigm case for joinder. In *State v. Bettinger* ... a
> sexual assault was joined with the defendant's attempt to bribe the same victim into
> dropping that very charge. In *Francis v. State* ... two abduction-assaults thirty-
> five days apart in the same two-block area shared one modus operandi. Nothing like
> that is alleged here.

**Why it works.** The factors are walked in order and each gets a clause. Then, rather
than stopping, the brief shows the court what a **real** joinder case looks like using
the leading authorities, so the gap between those cases and this one is visible rather
than asserted. Then the closing line: "What Counts 1-4 share with Counts 5-13 is Mr.
Doe's name, and nothing more."

**The move to steal:** after distinguishing adverse authority, use it affirmatively.
Show what the rule was made for; the distance from that to your facts is the argument.

## 7. The anticipated response, answered on two levels

> Counsel anticipates the State may respond that the June 1 arrest occurred while
> officers were attempting to locate Mr. Doe on warrants issued during the
> domestic-violence investigation. The complaint nowhere alleges the circumstances of
> the June 1 arrest ... **And even if such facts were later pleaded or proved**, they
> would establish only a temporal and procedural link, that Mr. Doe was arrested
> while wanted. They would not supply the substantive, evidentiary connection the
> statute and *Salinas* ... contemplate.

**Why it works.** The response is named specifically, not strawmanned. It is answered
first on the record ("nowhere alleges") and then on the merits ("and even if"). The
second answer means winning the first is optional. The closing line reframes the
State's best fact as trivial: "The happenstance of what an arrestee is alleged to
have been carrying on the day officers found him supplies nothing of the kind."

**The move to steal:** the two-level answer. It is not in the record, **and** it would
not matter if it were.

## 8. The concession

> Mr. Doe recognizes that relief requires a showing of "substantial prejudice,"
> *id.*; see also *State v. Linton*, 2010 WI App 129, ¶ 21 ... (requiring "a higher
> degree of prejudice, or certainty of prejudice"). **This record makes that
> showing.**

**Why it works.** It concedes a standard the argument had to meet anyway, cites the
case that states it most demandingly against himself, and then turns immediately with
a four-word sentence. Cost: nothing. Benefit: the court now trusts his account of the
law.

**The move to steal:** state the standard against yourself, in its strongest form,
then meet it.

## 9. The argument that makes the others unnecessary comes last

> Finally, if the Court agrees that Counts 1-4 do not meet the criteria of
> § 971.12(1), prejudice need not be demonstrated at all: "if the offenses do not meet
> the criteria for joinder, it is presumed that the defendant will be prejudiced by a
> joint trial." *State v. Leach* ...

**Why it works.** This point, placed first, would have made Section B look like
surplus. Placed last, it is a gift to a judge who has already been persuaded by
Section A: you do not have to do the prejudice analysis at all. It also quietly gives
the court the cheapest route to granting the motion.

The section closes on judicial self-interest: severing now "spares the parties and the
Court the after-the-fact harmless-error litigation *Leach* contemplates."

**The move to steal:** end with the point that lets the court rule for you with the
least work.

## 10. The conclusion mirrors the motion

> Mr. Doe respectfully asks the Court to sever Counts 1-4 from Counts 5-13 ... and
> to order that Counts 1-4 proceed, if at all, to a separate preliminary examination
> and a separate trial. In addition or in the alternative, he asks the Court to
> dismiss Counts 1-4 without prejudice ... and to grant such other relief as justice
> requires. Wis. Stat. § 971.12(3). He requests a hearing on this motion before any
> preliminary examination is conducted.

**Why it works.** It tracks the enumerated relief in the motion almost word for word,
so there is no gap for an opponent to exploit or a court to misread. The last sentence
asks for timing relief that appears nowhere else and that the court would not think of
on its own.

**The move to steal:** the conclusion is the motion's relief, restated. If they differ,
one of them is wrong.

## Things this motion does not do

Worth noticing, because the absences are deliberate.

- No section titled "Legal Standard." The standard for each argument appears at the
  top of that argument, where it is used.
- No procedural history beyond the timeliness paragraph.
- No footnotes. Adverse authority and anticipated responses are in the body.
- No adjectives about the State's conduct.
- No string cites. Where two cases are cited together, the second is doing distinct
  work (`accord`, or a Court of Appeals case following a supreme court case).
- No block quote longer than three lines except where the quoted language is the
  thing being construed.
- No claim that anything is "clear," "plain," or "obvious."

## The one number worth remembering

Two arguments. Three subarguments under the first, four under the second. Thirteen
counts, three facts, seven factors walked. The whole motion is roughly nine pages.
It could have been twenty and it would have been worse.
