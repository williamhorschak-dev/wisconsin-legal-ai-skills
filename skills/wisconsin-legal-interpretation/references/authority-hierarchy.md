# Wisconsin Authority Hierarchy — What Binds, What Persuades, What May Not Be Cited

Generic legal reasoning treats "the most recent case" as the strongest case. Wisconsin does not
work that way. Getting the hierarchy wrong produces confident answers built on decisions that
bind nobody, or citations that are affirmatively improper.

---

## 1. The stack

| Rank | Authority | Binds |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | U.S. Constitution; federal statutes and treaties within their scope | Everyone, on federal questions |
| 2 | U.S. Supreme Court on federal law | All Wisconsin courts |
| 3 | Wisconsin Constitution | All Wisconsin courts, on state law |
| 4 | Wisconsin statutes | All Wisconsin courts |
| 5 | Wisconsin Supreme Court published decisions | All Wisconsin courts |
| 6 | Wisconsin Court of Appeals **published** decisions | All Wisconsin circuit courts, and the court of appeals itself statewide |
| 7 | Wisconsin Administrative Code (valid rules within enabling authority) | Binding as law; on judicial review under ch. 227, agency *interpretation* of law gets no deference, § 227.57(11); municipal certiorari review has its own standards, including deference to a municipality's reasonable interpretation of its own ordinance, *Grycowski v. Milwaukee Emps.' Ret. Sys./Annuity & Pension Bd.*, 2021 WI App 7, ¶ 30 & n.2 |
| 8 | County / municipal ordinances within the enacting body's power and not preempted | Within that jurisdiction |
| — | Court of appeals **unpublished** decisions | Generally may **not** be cited as precedent or authority — § 809.23(3)(a); narrow persuasive exception in (b) |
| — | Circuit court decisions | No precedential value anywhere, including on the same judge |
| — | Seventh Circuit and other federal courts of appeals, on **federal** law | **Persuasive only** in Wisconsin state courts |
| — | Federal district courts | Persuasive only |
| — | Other states' decisions, Restatements, treatises, law reviews, Wisconsin Attorney General opinions | Persuasive only; AG opinions carry practical weight with agencies but bind no court |

Two entries in that table are the ones a generic assistant gets wrong by default: **the Seventh Circuit does
not bind Wisconsin state courts**, and **circuit court decisions bind nothing**, including
decisions in the same case before the same judge (law of the case is a different doctrine with
its own rules).

## 2. Published versus unpublished — Wis. Stat. § 809.23(3)

Verbatim:

**`[UNVERIFIED]` Retrieve all three paragraphs before relying on any of them.** This file gives
the operative clause of each, transcribed at build time. The qualifications in (b) in particular
are longer than what is reproduced here.

**(a)**

> "An unpublished opinion may not be cited in any court of this state as precedent or authority,
> except to support a claim of claim preclusion, issue preclusion, or the law of the case, and
> except as provided in par. (b)."

**(b)**

> "In addition to the purposes specified in par. (a), an unpublished opinion issued on or after
> July 1, 2009, that is authored by a member of a three-judge panel or by a single judge under
> s. 752.31 (2) may be cited for its persuasive value. A per curiam opinion, memorandum opinion,
> summary disposition order, or other order is not an authored opinion for purposes of this
> subsection."

Par. (b) continues that such opinions are not binding precedent and a court need not distinguish
one.

**(c)**

> "A party citing an unpublished opinion shall file and serve a copy of the opinion with the
> brief or other paper in which the opinion is cited."

**Checklist before any court of appeals decision enters a memo or a filing:**

1. Is it published? Check the citation form — a Wisconsin public-domain cite like `2019 WI App
   14` indicates publication; `No. 2018AP1234, unpublished slip op.` does not. Verify against
   wicourts.gov rather than the citation's appearance.
2. If unpublished: is it **on or after July 1, 2009**, and authored by a three-judge panel member or a
   single judge under § 752.31(2)? Both conditions, not either.
3. If yes to both: it may be cited **for persuasive value only** — never as "the court held" or
   "Wisconsin law provides." Say "persuasive only" in the sentence that cites it.
4. If no: it may be cited only for claim preclusion, issue preclusion, or law of the case.
5. Either way, § 809.23(3)(c) requires filing and serving a copy. Add it to the copies-to-file
   list. Forgetting this is a routine, avoidable embarrassment.
6. **Check the express exclusions.** Paragraph (b) excludes a per curiam opinion, memorandum
   opinion, summary disposition order, or other order from its definition of an authored
   opinion. This is an express rule, not a rebuttable inference about authorship. Read the
   opinion's designation and the current rule; for these Wisconsin Court of Appeals
   dispositions, only paragraph (a)'s preclusion and law-of-the-case uses remain. The exclusion
   appears in [Supreme Court Order 08-02, 2009 WI 2](https://www.wicourts.gov/sc/rulhear/DisplayDocument.pdf?content=pdf&seqNo=35116).
7. **Which court are you filing in?** Par. (a)'s prohibition reaches "any court of this state,"
   so it governs a circuit court brief as squarely as an appellate one. Par. (c)'s copy-filing
   duty is phrased for "the brief or other paper" and sits in ch. 809, the rules of **appellate**
   procedure. Whether (c) binds a circuit-court filing on its own terms is a real question this
   file does not resolve. **File the copy anyway** — it costs nothing, it is what an appellate
   court requires, and a circuit judge handed a copy of the cited opinion is never worse off —
   but do not tell a reader that (c) applies in circuit court as though that were settled. Check
   the county's local rules, which sometimes say.

## 3. `[MANDATORY RULE]` Conflicting Court of Appeals authority

**The court of appeals is one unitary court with four districts, not four courts.** No district
may overrule another, and the court may not overrule itself. Only the supreme court may overrule,
modify, or withdraw language from a published court of appeals opinion. *Cook v. Cook*, 208
Wis. 2d 166, 186, 189-90, 560 N.W.2d 246 (1997).

*State v. Jensen*, 2021 WI 27, ¶ 11, restates that holding and says *Cook* does not apply to the
law of the case; *Mitchell v. Buesgen*, 2024 WI App 14, n.17, treats the court of appeals as
"bound to follow" its precedent as explained in *Cook*. Neither changes the rule above.

Buesgen, 2024 WI App 14, n.17, https://www.wicourts.gov/ca/opinion/DisplayDocument.pdf?content=pdf&seqNo=767860; read 2026-10-12. No citator content. -->

**Cite *Cook* by page, not by paragraph.** It is a 1997 decision predating public-domain
paragraph numbering. The pincites the supreme court itself uses: **208 Wis. 2d at 186** (unitary
court; statewide precedential effect), **at 189** ("must speak with a unified voice";
"predictability, certainty and finality"; risk that litigants "litigate issues multiple times in
the four districts"), **at 189-90** (only the supreme court may overrule), **at 190** (the two
options).

**There is no newest-wins rule, and no free choice between conflicting decisions.** A later panel
that purports to overrule an earlier published decision acted without power, so its overruling
language is a nullity and the **earlier** published decision stands. *State v. Johnson*, 2004 WI
94, ¶ 17, 273 Wis. 2d 626, 681 N.W.2d 901:

> "The court in Kuehl stated that Haseltine ... and Jackson ... conflicted, and it concluded that
> because of that perceived conflict, it was 'free to follow the decision which [it]
> conclude[s] is correct.' Kuehl, 199 Wis. 2d at 149. The court gave no authority for this
> proposition; and we know of none. Therefore, any language in Kuehl that purports to overrule or
> undermine Jackson should not be accorded any precedential value in the future."

**This is the right *Johnson*:** *State v. Victor K. Johnson*, 2004 WI 94, No. 02-2793-CR. Not
*State v. Johnson*, 2023 WI 39, and not any court of appeals *Johnson*.

**Never advise that the most recent court of appeals decision controls.** That is the federal
circuit intuition and it is wrong in Wisconsin.

**Harmonize before declaring a conflict.** Apparently conflicting decisions should be read
together where they can be. A "conflict" that dissolves on a careful reading is not one.

**Certification when disposition turns on a perceived conflict.** *Cook*, 208 Wis. 2d at 190
gives the court of appeals two and only two options: certify to the supreme court under Wis.
Stat. (Rule) § 809.61, explaining that it believes a prior case was wrongly decided; or decide
the appeal adhering to the prior case while stating that belief. A **perceived** conflict
suffices; the panel need not first establish an irreconcilable one. *Johnson*, 2004 WI 94, ¶ 18:

> "when a perceived conflict arises ... a certification to this court that points out the
> perceived conflict will best serve the public interest and will also aid this court in its law
> developing and clarifying function. ... However, overruling an earlier court of appeals decision
> is not an option."

The current restatement is *Wisconsin Voter Alliance v. Secord*, 2025 WI 2, ¶¶ 32-34, 36, 39,
414 Wis. 2d 348, 15 N.W.3d 872, which also rejects "fine distinctions between arguments and
assuming additional or different facts" as a way around *Cook*.

### 3.1 What the *litigant* actually does about it

*Cook* states the **court's** options. A party asking "so what do I file" needs a different list,
and this is the step a *Cook* recitation most often leaves out.

**If the earlier decision helps you:**

1. **Say the later panel's overruling language is a nullity, and say why** — *Cook* at 189-90,
   *Johnson* ¶ 17. Do not merely assert that both decisions are "live"; name which one survives
   and on what authority.
2. **Check the interval first.** Did the supreme court decide anything between the two decisions
   that the later panel was faithfully applying? If it did, *Cook* does nothing for you, because
   the later panel was following a superior court rather than overruling a peer. Run the
   `sources.md` § 3 supreme-court check before building the argument.
3. **Ask the court of appeals to certify.** Wis. Stat. (Rule) § 809.61 is written as the court's
   mechanism, but nothing stops a party from identifying the perceived conflict in its brief and
   asking the court to certify. A **perceived** conflict is the standard (*Johnson* ¶ 18), which
   is a low bar to articulate.

**If the later decision hurts you and the panel simply followed it:**

4. **The remedy is the supreme court, not the court of appeals.** Only the supreme court can
   withdraw the language. **Wis. Stat. (Rule) § 809.62 governs the petition for review**, and an
   intra-court-of-appeals conflict is squarely within the criteria the rule lists. Retrieve the
   current text of § 809.62 for the criteria and, critically, **the deadline** — a petition
   deadline is jurisdictional and is not computed from this file (see SKILL.md's scope
   exclusions).
5. **Preserve it below.** An argument not made in the court of appeals is a harder petition. If
   the conflict is visible before the appellate briefs are filed, brief it there.

`[UNVERIFIED]` § 809.61 and § 809.62 are named here by number and subject only; retrieve both
before relying on their contents or their deadlines.

**Fragmented supreme court decisions: read the mandate and count votes on each proposition.**
Do not assume the federal *Marks* approach governs a Wisconsin decision, or that every fractured
opinion automatically leaves the decision below intact. In *State v. Lynch*, 2016 WI 66,
the opening mandate and footnote 1 explain that the court of appeals decision remained because
no majority agreed on the same rationale or result. That disposition must be read from the
decision, not inferred merely from the number of separate opinions.
[Official opinion](https://www.wicourts.gov/sc/opinion/DisplayDocument.pdf?content=pdf&seqNo=171644),
read 2026-09-20; not Shepardized or KeyCited for this update. For minority vote pooling, retrieve
*State v. Gustafson*, 121 Wis. 2d 459, 359 N.W.2d 920 (1985), and current Wisconsin treatment
before asserting a rule. `[UNVERIFIED]` *Gustafson* has not been rechecked against an official
copy for this update. Separate propositions supported by a majority from reasoning that did not
attract one, and state any unresolved precedential question explicitly.


## 4. Dicta — narrower in Wisconsin than the reflex

Two formulations coexist:

- The narrower-dicta view: a discussion of a question **"germane to … the controversy"** is not
  dictum, even if unnecessary to the outcome. ***Zarder v. Humana Ins. Co.***, 2010 WI 35.
- The broader view: dictum is **"a statement or language expressed in a court's opinion which
  extends beyond the facts in the case and is broader than necessary."** ***State v. Sartin***,
  200 Wis. 2d 47.

The operational rule from *Zarder* matters most: **the court of appeals may not dismiss language
from a Wisconsin Supreme Court opinion as dicta**, because doing so would modify the supreme
court's language — which *Cook* forbids. So "that's just dicta" is usually a losing move against
supreme court language, and a memo that relies on it is fragile.

Before characterizing anything as dicta: retrieve *Zarder* and *Sartin*, quote the formulation
you are applying, apply it, and state what happens to your argument if the court disagrees.

## 5. Weight and posture modifiers

Even binding authority varies in force. Record these in the authority table:

- **Standard of review.** Statutory interpretation and constitutional questions are reviewed
  de novo; findings of fact are reviewed for clear error under Wis. Stat. § 805.17(2);
  discretionary determinations are reviewed for erroneous exercise of discretion. Which one
  applies frequently decides the case and the generic default is to skip it. Name it, quote the
  authority for it, and let it shape the prediction lens.
- **Procedural posture.** A holding on summary judgment, on a motion to dismiss, and after trial
  are not interchangeable, and a case cited for a proposition it announced in a different posture
  is a weak citation waiting to be attacked.
- **Concurrences, dissents, lead opinions.** A Wisconsin Supreme Court "lead opinion" not joined
  by a majority does not bind. Count the votes. A three-two-two split may have no majority
  rationale at all — say so rather than citing the lead opinion as the holding.
- **Subsequent history.** Reversed, vacated, overruled in part, superseded by statute, limited by
  a later case, or abrogated by a rule change. Never assume; run a citator, or disclose that none was run and cap the confidence (`sources.md` § 4).
- **Superseded by statute** is the quiet one. A case correctly stating the law of a former
  statutory text is not law now. Compare the statutory text the case construed against the text
  in force at your operative date, and quote both when they differ.

## 6. Ordinances, agency materials, and the bottom of the stack

- **A valid ordinance is law** within its jurisdiction, but it must clear both the enabling-power
  and the preemption questions in `canons.md` §6 before it does any work in a memo.
- **Agency guidance documents, manuals, FAQs, bulletins, and staff letters are not law** and get
  no deference — § 227.57(11) plus the guidance-document rules in § 227.112. They are useful as
  evidence of practice and as admissions, and should be labeled as exactly that.
- **Wisconsin Attorney General opinions** are persuasive only. They bind no court, but agencies
  and municipal officers follow them in practice, which can be worth more than precedential
  weight in a pre-litigation posture. Label them accurately.
- **Jury instructions (Wis JI–Civil, Wis JI–Criminal)** are drafted by committees, not enacted.
  They are persuasive and widely followed; they are not authority for what a statute means, and
  a memo citing an instruction rather than the statute has skipped a step.
- **Judicial Council notes and committee comments** accompanying procedural rules carry
  interpretive weight; retrieve and quote them where a procedural question is close.

## 7. The federal overlay

- Wisconsin courts follow the U.S. Supreme Court on federal questions and treat all lower federal
  courts as **persuasive only**, the Seventh Circuit included. Write "persuasive."
- Federal courts sitting in diversity or supplemental jurisdiction apply Wisconsin substantive law
  as the Wisconsin Supreme Court has stated it, and predict where it has not. Certification under
  Wis. Stat. § 821.01 is the mechanism for an unsettled question.
- **Federal citability is a separate regime, and the two subdivisions do different work.**
  **FRAP 32.1(a) governs permitted citation**: "A court may not prohibit or restrict the citation
  of federal judicial opinions, orders, judgments, or other written dispositions that have been:
  (i) designated as 'unpublished,' 'not for publication,' 'non-precedential,' 'not precedent,' or
  the like; and (ii) issued on or after January 1, 2007." **FRAP 32.1(b) governs copies**: "If a
  party cites a federal judicial opinion, order, judgment, or other written disposition that is
  not available in a publicly accessible electronic database, the party must file and serve a
  copy." Note that (a) binds **courts**, does not confer precedential force, and reaches only
  dispositions issued on or after January 1, 2007; and that (b) is triggered by database
  **unavailability**, not by unpublished status. **Neither applies in a Wisconsin state court**.
  Do not infer that § 809.23(3) prohibits unpublished federal or other-state opinions there:
  its noncitation restriction concerns Wisconsin Court of Appeals decisions. *Predick v.
  O'Connor*, 2003 WI App 46, ¶ 12 n.7, explains that § 809.23(3) does not prohibit citation of
  unpublished opinions from other jurisdictions, quoting *Brandt v. LIRC*, 160 Wis. 2d 353,
  363, 466 N.W.2d 673 (Ct. App. 1991).
  [Official opinion](https://www.wicourts.gov/html/ca/02/02-0503.htm), checked 2026-09-20;
  recheck its treatment and the receiving court's rules before use. Such opinions carry
  persuasive weight only. And (a)'s protection of
  *citation* says nothing about *weight*: the circuit's own local rule still governs how a
  nonprecedential disposition is treated. Check the current Seventh Circuit rule; do not carry
  the Wisconsin § 809.23(3) analysis across the line, and do not carry FRAP 32.1 back into state
  court.
- **Preclusion across systems** follows the rendering court's law. A Wisconsin judgment's
  preclusive effect in federal court is measured by Wisconsin preclusion law, and vice versa.
  This is a place where a plausible-sounding shortcut produces a wrong answer.

## 8. The authority-classification block

Every authority entering a memo carries this block in the authority table. No exceptions, and an
authority missing a field is flagged rather than quietly included.

```
Citation:        <full Wisconsin citation form>
Court / level:   <Wis. / Wis. Ct. App. published / Wis. Ct. App. unpublished / Cir. Ct. / 7th Cir. / ...>
Date:            <decision date>
Binding on:      <this forum? binding | persuasive | may not be cited as authority>
Citability:      <published | unpublished post-7/1/2009 persuasive-only | unpublished restricted>
Copy to file:    <yes/no — § 809.23(3)(c) in state court; FRAP 32.1(b) in federal>
Posture:         <MTD | SJ | post-trial | certiorari | ...>
Standard of rev: <de novo | clearly erroneous | erroneous exercise of discretion>
Votes:           <majority | lead opinion, no majority | per curiam>
Subsequent hist: <citator result, source, date checked>
Statute vintage: <text construed vs. text in force at operative date — same / differs how>
Retrieved:       <path in sources/ | URL | date>
How found:       <search-for-proposition | citator | cited-in-brief | recalled-and-confirmed*>
```

`recalled-and-confirmed` is permitted but is **always** flagged to the requester regardless of how well
the authority checks out. Confirming that a remembered case exists is not confirming that it
holds what you remembered. That gap is where hallucinated law survives verification.
