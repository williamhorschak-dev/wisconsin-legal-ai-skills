## 1. The controlling framework

**Authority, in order.** *Service Employees Int'l Union Healthcare Wisconsin v. WERC*, 2025 WI
29, ¶¶ 8-12, 416 Wis. 2d 688, 22 N.W.3d 876, is the current statement of the sequence, and
*Brekke v. Midwest Medical Insurance Co.*, 2026 WI 29, ¶ 20, is to the same effect. Both clarify
rather than replace *State ex rel. Kalal v. Circuit Court for Dane County*, 2004 WI 58, 271
Wis. 2d 633, 681 N.W.2d 110.

**One note on the citations, because it looks wrong and is not.** *SEIU* and *Brekke* carry the
**same opinion number in consecutive terms** — 2025 WI 29 and 2026 WI 29. Wisconsin's
public-domain numbering restarts each year, so this is ordinary; it is also the shape that makes
a reader suspect a fabricated cite, which is why it is flagged here rather than left to surprise
someone mid-brief. Verify both against the court's own opinion pages before either appears in a
filing, as you would any authority — that is rule 1, not a special precaution for these two.

**Official-source check, 2026-09-20.** *SEIU*, 2025 WI 29, No. 2024AP717, decided 6/27/2025:
¶¶ 8, 10-12 read in the [court's opinion](https://www.wicourts.gov/sc/order/DisplayDocImage.pdf?docId=977376).
*Brekke*, 2026 WI 29, No. 2023AP498, decided 7/10/2026: ¶ 20 read in the
[court's opinion](https://www.wicourts.gov/sc/opinion/DisplayDocument.pdf?content=pdf&seqNo=1143901)
and the quotation below confirmed. This verifies those passages, not subsequent treatment;

### 1.0 The sequence, as SEIU states it

*SEIU* ¶ 8, verbatim:

> "Intrinsic sources include the statutory text at issue, related statutes and phrases, a
> statute's place within the statutory structure, its stated or textually manifest purpose, and
> statutory history. Kalal, 271 Wis. 2d 633, ¶¶45, 46, 48-49, 52 n.9. Extrinsic sources, by
> contrast, are 'interpretive resources outside the statutory text—typically items of legislative
> history.' Id., ¶50. Intrinsic sources are primary in determining the plain meaning of a statute,
> while extrinsic sources are secondary and used to confirm that plain meaning or resolve any
> ambiguity. Id., ¶51."

*SEIU* ¶ 10, verbatim:

> "Our statement in Kalal does not mean statutory interpretation begins and ends with a myopic
> focus on the singular statutory provision in question. Rather, statutory interpretation begins
> and is usually complete only after a full consideration of all relevant intrinsic sources."

*SEIU* ¶ 11, verbatim, on the distinction the reviewer flagged:

> "SEIU also misses the mark on the role of statutory history, which 'involves comparing the
> statute with its prior versions.' Id., ¶20. Since statutory history analyzes enacted law, it is
> an intrinsic source that is 'part and parcel of a plain meaning interpretation.' Banuelos v.
> Univ. of Wis. Hosps. & Clinics Auth., 2023 WI 25, ¶25, 406 Wis. 2d 439, 988 N.W.2d 627.
> Statutory history is therefore part of the statutory context and, where relevant, should be
> examined when a court determines the meaning of a statutory provision."

*SEIU* ¶ 12, verbatim:

> "In short, SEIU's argument—that statutory history should not be consulted when the 'plain
> meaning' of the disputed provision is unambiguous—is simply mistaken and inconsistent with
> decades of statutory interpretation cases from this court. Rather, all intrinsic sources—text,
> context, and structure—are essential components of a plain meaning analysis."

*Brekke* ¶ 20, verbatim:

> "'[S]tatutory interpretation begins and is usually complete only after a full consideration of
> all relevant intrinsic sources' including 'the statutory text at issue, related statutes and
> phrases, a statute's place within the statutory structure, its stated or textually manifest
> purpose, and statutory history.' Serv. Emp. Int'l Union Healthcare Wis. v. WERC, 2025 WI 29,
> ¶¶8, 10, 416 Wis. 2d 688, 22 N.W.3d 876."

**Statutory history is intrinsic. Legislative history is extrinsic.** Prior versions, amending
acts, repeals, and renumbering are consulted **before** any ambiguity finding. Drafting records,
committee notes, and LRB files are consulted only to confirm a plain meaning already reached or
to resolve an ambiguity. *Kalal* ¶¶ 50-51 and ¶ 52 n.9 (drawing the line at "previously enacted
and repealed statutory provisions").

**Intrinsic canons vs. substantive canons.** Intrinsic canons run inside the plain-meaning pass:
ordinary meaning with the technical-term exception (¶ 45); whole-text and in-context reading;
reading in relation to surrounding statutes; surplusage; absurdity avoidance (¶ 46); scope,
context and purpose where ascertainable from text and structure (¶ 48). Substantive canons apply
only to an ambiguity surviving that whole analysis. *State v. Guarnero*, 2015 WI 72, ¶ 27: the
rule of lenity applies only "if a 'grievous ambiguity' remains after a court has determined the
statute's meaning by considering statutory language, context, structure and purpose, such that
the court must 'simply guess' at the meaning of the statute." `[UNVERIFIED]` *Guarnero* reporter
cite.

**An enacted construction directive is itself intrinsic statutory text.** Read it in Stage A,
within its stated scope, even if it directs liberal construction of a remedial statute. This
routing follows *SEIU*'s treatment of statutory text and related statutes as intrinsic sources;
it does not establish that a directive overrides other clear statutory language. Retrieve the
operative directive and controlling decisions applying it. If its effect remains unresolved,
identify that issue rather than treating the directive as either automatically controlling or
unavailable until ambiguity.

**Never stop at plain meaning without (a) searching for a controlling judicial construction of
the same language and (b) checking current treatment.** A published construction binds however
plain the text reads to you.

**Kalal, the original:** *State ex rel. Kalal v. Circuit Court for Dane County*, 2004 WI 58, 271 Wis. 2d
633, 681 N.W.2d 110. Official text: `wicourts.gov/sc/opinions/02/pdf/02-2490.pdf` (Case No.
02-2490-W).

### 1.1 Purpose

> "the purpose of statutory interpretation is to determine what the statute means so that it may
> be given its full, proper, and intended effect." (¶44) **Re-retrieved and confirmed verbatim
> 2026-08-29** against `wicourts.gov/sc/opinions/02/pdf/02-2490.pdf`.

> "Judicial deference to the policy choices enacted into law by the legislature requires that
> statutory interpretation focus primarily on the language of the statute." (¶44)

Note what this displaces: the common assistant default of opening with "the purpose of the statute
is to protect consumers, so…" Wisconsin puts purpose *inside* the text, not ahead of it. Purpose
reasoning that does not run through the words is not Wisconsin method.

### 1.2 Start with the text

> "Statutory interpretation 'begins with the language of the statute.'" (¶45)

> "Statutory language is given its common, ordinary, and accepted meaning, except that technical
> or specially-defined words or phrases are given their technical or special definitional
> meaning." (¶45)

> "If the meaning of the statute is plain, we ordinarily stop the inquiry." (¶45)

### 1.3 Context and structure are part of plain meaning

> "Context is important to meaning." (¶46)

> "Therefore, statutory language is interpreted in the context in which it is used; not in
> isolation but as part of a whole; in relation to the language of surrounding or closely-related
> statutes; and reasonably, to avoid absurd or unreasonable results." (¶46)

This is the single most misapplied sentence in Wisconsin practice, in both directions. It is not
a license to consult anything you like before finding ambiguity — but it does mean surrounding
sections, the statute's own structure, and the avoidance of absurd results are **intrinsic**, and
are consulted at step one, not after a finding of ambiguity. What counts as intrinsic versus
extrinsic is actively contested; see §1.6.

### 1.4 Ambiguity

> "a statute is ambiguous if it is capable of being understood by reasonably well-informed
> persons in two or more senses." (¶47)

The test is not "the parties disagree." A litigant's strained reading does not create ambiguity;
the two senses must each be available to a reasonably well-informed reader. Say in the memo
which two senses, in whose mouth, and why each is reasonable — or concede the text is plain and
argue the other side of the case.

### 1.5 Extrinsic sources

> "Where statutory language is unambiguous, there is no need to consult extrinsic sources of
> interpretation, such as legislative history." (¶46)

> "Thus, as a general matter, legislative history need not be and is not consulted except to
> resolve an ambiguity in the statutory language, although legislative history is sometimes
> consulted to confirm or verify a plain-meaning interpretation." (¶51)

The confirm-or-verify clause is the workhorse and the trap. It permits legislative history to
*confirm* a plain reading; it does not permit history to *establish* one. A memo that reaches
its conclusion through a drafting file and then cites ¶51 has inverted the rule, and the red team
should catch it.

Wisconsin legislative history materials, and where they live, are in `sources.md` § 5.

## 2. The statutory rules of construction — Wisconsin's own

Wisconsin has codified construction rules. They are law, not commentary, and they beat any
canon recalled from a treatise.

### 2.1 Wis. Stat. § 990.01 — words and phrases

Introductory language:

> "In the construction of Wisconsin laws the words and phrases which follow shall be construed as
> indicated unless such construction would produce a result inconsistent with the manifest intent
> of the legislature"

Subsection (1), the general rule:

> "All words and phrases shall be construed according to common and approved usage; but technical
> words and phrases and others that have a peculiar meaning in the law shall be construed
> according to such meaning."

**Always check § 990.01 for a statutory definition of the disputed word before reaching for a
dictionary.** Chapter 990 defines a long list of recurring terms, and a defined term ends the
ordinary-meaning inquiry. Then check the subject chapter's own definitions section, which
overrides § 990.01 within its scope. Order of operations: (a) definition inside the section
itself; (b) definitions section of the chapter; (c) § 990.01; (d) common and approved usage,
i.e. a general dictionary contemporaneous with enactment; (e) for technical or legal terms of
art, a legal dictionary.

### 2.2 Wis. Stat. § 990.001, construction of laws

**`[MANDATORY RULE]` The chapter is itself qualified.** § 990.001 opens:

> "In construing Wisconsin laws the following rules shall be observed unless construction in
> accordance with a rule would produce a result inconsistent with the manifest intent of the
> legislature:"

So every rule in § 990.001, including the title rule, yields to manifest legislative intent.

**Statutory titles, the qualified rule.** § 990.001(6):

> "The titles to subchapters, sections, subsections, paragraphs and subdivisions of the statutes
> and history notes are not part of the statutes."

That is not a categorical bar on looking at a title. The controlling qualification is *Noffke v.
Bakke*, 2009 WI 10, ¶ 25:

> "In addition, a title may not be used to alter the meaning of a statute or create an ambiguity
> where no ambiguity existed."

So: a title is not part of the statute and **may not be used to alter meaning or manufacture
ambiguity**. Wisconsin authority permitting affirmative use of a title to resolve a genuine doubt
rests on *In Interest of C.D.M.*, 125 Wis. 2d 170, 370 N.W.2d 287 (Ct. App. 1985) `[UNVERIFIED]`
retrieved only from an LRB annotation; pull the opinion before citing it.

**Chapter headings are not covered.** § 990.001(6) names subchapter, section, subsection,
paragraph, and subdivision titles. It does not name **chapter** headings, and no authority
resolving them was located. Treat that as open, not as settled either way.


### 2.3 Time computation — two regimes, do not mix them

- **General statutory computation:** Wis. Stat. § 990.001(4).
- **Civil procedure computation:** Wis. Stat. § 801.15, including the § 801.15(5) add-on periods
  for service by mail and the treatment of service after 5 p.m. Appellate deadlines have their
  own rules in ch. 809.

A deadline answer that does not name which regime it used, and does not state the resulting
calendar date with the day of the week, is not finished. Deadline errors are the most expensive
interpretive errors in practice and the easiest to make silently.

### 2.4 Finding the version in force on a past date

`[UNVERIFIED]` Every provision named in this subsection is named by number and subject only.
Retrieve each before relying on it.

Most "which version applies" questions are answered mechanically, before any retroactivity
doctrine is reached. Work in this order:

1. **Read the section's history note.** Each section on docs.legis ends with a bracketed trail of
   the Acts that touched it. That trail is how you learn *which* Act changed *which* subsection
   and when. § 990.001(6) says history notes are not part of the statutes — true, and irrelevant
   here: you are using the note as a finding aid, not citing it as law.
2. **Find the amending Act's own effective-date and initial-applicability sections.** These are
   the final numbered SECTIONs of the Act itself, and **they override the default.** An Act that
   says it "first applies to actions commenced on the effective date of this subsection" answers
   the question outright. Read the Act, not a summary of it.
3. **Only if the Act is silent, apply the default effective date.** Wis. Stat. **§ 991.11** — the
   general rule that an act takes effect the day after publication. Retrieve its current text;
   do not paraphrase it from here.
4. **Pull the text as it stood.** Free route: the year-scoped statute archives on the aggregators
   carry prior editions — Justia's pattern is
   `law.justia.com/codes/wisconsin/<year>/chapter-<N>/section-<N-N>/`, and a prior-year page
   carries a banner reading that a newer version exists, which is the confirmation you want.
   Paid route: an authenticated database's superseded-text view (`sources.md` § 2.4). Either way,
   record the edition and the banner in the source file header.
5. **Diff the two versions and quote both** where the difference is the point. A memo that says
   "the statute was amended" without showing the words that changed is not usable.
6. **In a criminal or forfeiture matter, raise the constitutional question.** Applying a later,
   harsher statute to earlier conduct implicates the ex post facto clauses — Wis. Const. art. I,
   § 12 and U.S. Const. art. I, § 10. This file states no rule about them; it states that they
   are the first thing to check and that they are not reached by the presumption against
   retroactivity.

**Remember that a biennial edition is not a version.** "Wis. Stat. § X (2021-22)" names an
edition; the text in force on a given day in that biennium may differ from it, because Acts take
effect continuously.

## 3. Canons — as Wisconsin actually uses them

Canons are tiebreakers, not premises. A memo that opens with a canon has usually skipped the
text. Each canon below gets used only after the text, structure, and closely-related statutes
have been worked, and each gets cited to a Wisconsin case applying it — **retrieve that case; do
not cite a canon to a treatise or to memory.**

### 3.1 Textual / intrinsic canons — available without an ambiguity finding

- Whole-text and closely-related-statutes reading — *Kalal* ¶46, quoted above.
- Avoidance of absurd or unreasonable results — *Kalal* ¶46. Wisconsin uses this narrowly:
  "unwise" or "harsh" is not "absurd."
- *Expressio unius est exclusio alterius* — an express list implies exclusion of the unlisted.
  Strong where the legislature enumerated; weak where the list reads as illustrative ("includes").
- *Ejusdem generis* / *noscitur a sociis* — general words following a list take color from the
  list; a word is known by its companions.
- The rule against surplusage — every word given effect if possible.
- Specific governs the general; later-enacted governs earlier where they truly conflict. Note
  the order: Wisconsin looks hard for a harmonizing reading first, because repeal by implication
  is disfavored.
- Consistent usage — the same term means the same thing across the chapter, absent a signal.
- "Shall" versus "may." Presumptively mandatory versus permissive, but Wisconsin has read
  "shall" as directory where the statute lacks a consequence for noncompliance and mandatory
  reading would defeat the provision. **This is fact-specific and heavily litigated; retrieve
  current authority every time rather than asserting the presumption.**

### 3.2 Substantive / policy-based canons — only after an ambiguity finding

- The generic judge-made maxim that remedial statutes are liberally construed belongs here,
  subject to the governing Wisconsin authority. Distinguish an **enacted** directive, such as
  Wis. Stat. § 421.102: retrieve and quote its operative text, and read it in Stage A as a related
  statute. Do not postpone enacted text merely because it concerns liberal construction, or
  assume that it overrides other clear language; check the decisions applying that directive.
- Statutes in derogation of the common law — the traditional strict-construction rule, and the
  places Wisconsin has abandoned it. Check before invoking.
- The rule of lenity in criminal statutes — Wisconsin applies it only after the ordinary tools
  fail to resolve genuine ambiguity, never as an opening move.
- Constitutional avoidance — a construction that avoids a serious constitutional question is
  preferred where the statute is fairly susceptible to it.
- Retroactivity — `[UNVERIFIED]`, and deliberately not stated as a rule here. The received
  framing is that statutes are presumed prospective and that a substantive/procedural
  characterization drives the analysis, but this file cites no Wisconsin case for either half and
  the area is contested. **Retrieve Wisconsin authority before asserting anything about
  retroactivity**, and see § 2.4 for the mechanical operative-date work that usually
  answers the question without reaching the presumption at all.

### 3.3 Recording a canon

**For each canon actually used, the memo records:** which canon, the Wisconsin case applying it
(retrieved, with pincite and verbatim quote), why the sequence reached it, and what the opposing
canon would produce. Canons come in pairs; a memo citing one and ignoring its opposite is
advocacy wearing a research memo's clothes, and the red team treats it as a finding.

## 4. Administrative rules

### 4.0 `[MANDATORY RULE]` Agency interpretations: no deference, but due weight

Two subsections operate together, and the skill must state both.

> **§ 227.57(11)** "Upon review of an agency action or decision, the court shall accord no
> deference to the agency's interpretation of law."

> **§ 227.57(10)** "Subject to sub. (11), upon such review due weight shall be accorded the
> experience, technical competence, and specialized knowledge of the agency involved, as well as
> discretionary authority conferred upon it."

They are not in tension: (10) is expressly subordinated to (11) by its own opening clause.
**(11) removes deference on questions of law** — the court decides legal meaning independently
and owes the agency nothing on it. **(10) preserves a persuasive, non-deferential role** for the
agency's experience, technical competence, and specialized knowledge, as input to the court's own
judgment. For judicial review governed by chapter 227, the statute controls, § 227.57(10)-(11).
*Tetra Tech EC, Inc. v. DOR*, 2018 WI 75, ¶ 108, states the majority's end to deference to
agency conclusions of law while giving "due weight" under § 227.57(10) to the agency's experience,
technical competence, and specialized knowledge. Paragraph 78 is lead-opinion reasoning (Kelly,
J., joined in full only by R.G. Bradley, J.; see n.4), not a majority holding. It puts the fit
this way:

DOR, 2018 WI 75, n.4 (sections joined by a majority), ¶ 78 (Section II.A.5, lead opinion), ¶ 108 (Section III, majority), https://www.wicourts.gov/sc/opinion/DisplayDocument.pdf?content=pdf&seqNo=214793; read 2026-10-12. -->

> "giving 'due weight' to an agency's experience, technical competence, and specialized knowledge
> will not 'oust the court as the ultimate authority or final arbiter' of the law. Instead, it
> said, 'due weight' means giving 'respectful, appropriate consideration to the agency's views'
> while the court exercises its independent judgment in deciding questions of law. We agree. 'Due
> weight' is a matter of persuasion, not deference."

**Do not report (11) alone.** A memo that says only "no deference" overstates the position and
misses the argument actually available to the agency. Cite § 227.57(11) first and *Tetra Tech*
second; the abolition now rests on statute, not only on the court's own decision.

**Provenance of the two Act citations, which are different provisions and are often conflated.**
Both are `[UNVERIFIED]` section numbers taken from secondary sources; **pull the enrolled Act
before citing either by section number.**

- **2017 Wis. Act 369, § 35** is cited for creating **§ 227.10(2g)** — "no agency may seek
  deference in any proceeding based on the agency's interpretation of any law." That is the
  companion prohibition addressed to agencies.
- **2017 Wis. Act 369, § 80** is cited for **§ 227.57(11)**, and it **amended** rather than
  created that subsection: 2015 Wis. Act 391 had created it in a narrower, property-owner-only
  form.
- **"2018 Wis. Act 369" does not exist.** The Act passed in a December 2018 extraordinary session
  but carries the 2017 session-year prefix. Writing 2018 is a citation error, not a variant.

*League of Women Voters of Wisconsin v. Evers*, 2019 WI 75, ¶¶ 2, 42, held that extraordinary
sessions do not violate the Wisconsin Constitution and that the December 2018 extraordinary
session was constitutional; the Court vacated the circuit court's order and remanded for
dismissal (mandate). The decision addressed the session, not every challenge to a particular
provision enacted in it.

The statutory abolition followed *Tetra Tech EC, Inc. v. DOR*, 2018 WI 75, which ended
great-weight deference. **Retrieve and read *Tetra Tech* before writing about how a court will
treat an agency's reading; do not summarize it from memory, and do not quote ¶ 78 above without
re-pulling it.**

**Posture note.** § 227.57 governs *judicial review of an agency decision under ch. 227*. Where
there is no decision yet — an agency asserting in correspondence that a permit is required, an
enforcement action not yet brought, a declaratory-judgment posture — say so, and do not present
§ 227.57(11) as though the review posture were established. In that posture the **first-line
argument is usually § 227.10(2m)**, below: whether the agency has the explicit authority it is
asserting at all, which is a stronger point than how much deference its reading would get.

Wisconsin Administrative Code chapters are interpreted with the same textual method, plus three
Wisconsin-specific overlays that reverse the federal instinct entirely.

**Explicit-authority requirement.** Wis. Stat. § 227.10(2m):

> "No agency may implement or enforce any standard, requirement, or threshold, including as a
> term or condition of any license issued by the agency, unless that standard, requirement, or
> threshold is explicitly required or explicitly permitted by statute or by a rule that has been
> promulgated in accordance with this subchapter, except as provided in s. 186.118 (2) (c) and
> (3) (b) 3. The governor, by executive order, may prescribe guidelines to ensure that rules are
> promulgated in compliance with this subchapter."

**Explicit does not mean specific or narrowly enumerated.** *Clean Wisconsin, Inc. v. DNR*,
2021 WI 71, ¶¶ 24-25, holds that an express grant can be broad and still satisfy § 227.10(2m).
The court states:

> "an agency may rely upon a grant of authority that is explicit but broad when undertaking
> agency action, and such an explicit but broad grant of authority complies with § 227.10(2m)."

¶ 25, [official opinion](https://www.wicourts.gov/sc/opinion/DisplayDocument.pdf?content=pdf&seqNo=386188),
read 2026-09-20; not Shepardized or KeyCited for this update. Read the actual enabling text and
the cases construing it before challenging a requirement. A manual, guidance document, or
internal practice cannot substitute for statutory or valid rule authority, but the breadth of
the enacted grant alone does not establish its absence.

**Guidance documents are not rules.** Wis. Stat. § 227.112 and the ch. 227 definition of "rule"
in § 227.01(13) — retrieve both. An agency position embodied in a guidance document, manual, FAQ,
or letter does not carry the force of a promulgated rule, and a requirement that should have gone
through rulemaking and did not is challengeable.

**Order of authority:** statute beats rule; a rule exceeding its enabling statute is invalid to
that extent. Always locate the enabling statute and quote it alongside the rule.

## 5. The Wisconsin Constitution

Wisconsin constitutional interpretation is its own method, not a mirror of federal doctrine.

- Wisconsin courts may and do read parallel Wisconsin provisions **more protectively** than their
  federal analogues. Never assume the state provision means what the federal one means; that
  assumption is the single most common error in state constitutional argument.
- The standard approach looks to the plain meaning of the text, the constitutional debates and
  practices of the era of adoption, and the earliest legislative interpretation as evidenced by
  the first law passed following adoption. **Retrieve a current case stating the framework and
  quote it**; do not recite the tripartite formulation from memory, and check whether the current
  court has restated it.
- Article I, § 3 (free speech and press), Article I, § 11 (searches and seizures), and Article
  I, § 9 (remedy for wrongs, certain remedy clause) all have Wisconsin-specific bodies of law
  worth checking before defaulting to the First and Fourth Amendments.
- Public records and open meetings sit on statutory footing (Wis. Stat. §§ 19.31–19.39,
  19.81–19.98) with a declared policy of openness. Quote the declaration-of-policy sections
  (§ 19.31, § 19.81(1)–(4)) directly; they carry interpretive weight the statute assigns them and
  they are frequently the strongest sentences available to a requester.

## 6. Ordinances and municipal authority

A county or municipal ordinance question is two questions: did the body have power to enact it,
and does state law displace it.

**Power.** Home rule for cities and villages: Wis. Const. art. XI, § 3, and Wis. Stat.
§ 62.11(5). Counties: Wis. Stat. § 59.03 (administrative home rule) and § 59.51, plus the
specific enabling statute for whatever the ordinance does — county power is narrower than
municipal home rule and the enabling statute matters more. Towns: ch. 60, narrower still.
**Retrieve the enabling provision and quote it.**

**Displacement — search for a subject-specific preemption statute FIRST.** Before running any
four-prong test, look for a statute that speaks to this exact subject. The Legislature has
repeatedly preempted named municipal subjects by statute — short-term and residential rentals,
landlord-tenant regulation, wage and benefit ordinances, firearms, and others — and where one of
those exists it resolves the question outright and the general test never runs. Search the
statutes for the subject matter, not just for "preemption." A four-prong analysis offered where a
subject-specific statute controls is a wrong answer dressed as a thorough one.

**The general test, only if no subject-specific statute applies.** Wisconsin's preemption
analysis asks whether the state has expressly withdrawn the power, whether the ordinance
logically conflicts with state legislation, whether it defeats the purpose of state legislation,
and whether it goes against the spirit of state legislation. **Retrieve a current Wisconsin case
stating this test, quote it, and pincite it** — the formulation and its application have moved,
and the "spirit" prong in particular is contested. Do not state the test from this file in a
filing.

**A "does it apply to my 2022 lease" question is three questions, not one.** (1) What did the
ordinance say then — § 2.4 above and `sources.md` § 6. (2) Is the subject preempted — above.
(3) Does the ordinance reach a relationship formed before it, or is there a prior-nonconforming-
use, vested-rights, or grandfathering provision? Zoning-type ordinances in particular interact
with Wis. Stat. § 62.23(7)(h) and its county analogues. `[UNVERIFIED]` — that statute is named by
number and subject only; retrieve it, and retrieve the ordinance's own transition provisions,
which are frequently where the answer actually is.

Ordinance text itself is the hardest primary source to retrieve reliably; see `sources.md` § 6.
An ordinance quoted from a third-party code publisher without a currency date is not verified.

## 7. Federal law in a Wisconsin matter

When a matter straddles both systems, the memo says, **question by question**, which sovereign's
interpretive rules govern.

- **State courts interpreting federal statutes** are bound by the U.S. Supreme Court and are
  *not* bound by lower federal court decisions, including the Seventh Circuit, though they may
  find them persuasive. Say "persuasive," not "controlling," and be precise about it — the
  distinction is worth real money in a brief.
- **Federal courts interpreting Wisconsin law** apply Wisconsin's interpretive method and are
  bound by the Wisconsin Supreme Court's reading of Wisconsin law; where it has not spoken, they
  predict. Wis. Stat. § 821.01 provides for certification of questions of Wisconsin law to the
  Wisconsin Supreme Court.
- **Preemption** runs the federal analysis (express, field, conflict), not the state-municipal
  test in §6. Do not blend the two vocabularies.
- **Consumer matters** commonly present both: FDCPA (15 U.S.C. §§ 1692–1692p) alongside the
  Wisconsin Consumer Act (chs. 421–427), which is broader in places and carries its own
  liberal-construction directive and its own remedies. Analyze them separately and then compare;
  a memo that treats the WCA as "Wisconsin's FDCPA" has already lost the parts that matter.
- **§ 1983 and immunity** doctrines are federal; the state-law analogues (Wis. Stat. § 895.46
  indemnification, governmental immunity under § 893.80(4), and the ministerial-duty exception)
  are separate and have their own Wisconsin case law.

## 8. The sequence, written out

Run this in order and write down each step, including the empty ones.

**The order matters and it is not the intuitive one.** Under *SEIU* ¶ 10, interpretation "is
usually complete only after a full consideration of all relevant intrinsic sources" — so the
plain-meaning determination comes **after** structure, related statutes, statutory history, the
construction statutes, the intrinsic canons, and any binding construction of the same words. A
sequence that declares plain meaning at step 3 and then reaches "the canons" only if it finds
ambiguity is the *pre-SEIU* misreading of *Kalal*, and it is the single most common way a
Wisconsin analysis goes wrong. Ambiguity is a **finding at the end of the intrinsic work**, not a
gate in front of it.

### Stage A — Intrinsic sources (all of them, before any plain-meaning call)

1. **Text.** Quote the operative language verbatim, in the version in force at the operative
   date, with the currency banner captured.
2. **Definitions.** Section → chapter → § 990.01 → common and approved usage → technical meaning
   for terms of art. Quote whichever one controls. Where the section borrows its conduct or its
   nouns from another chapter by cross-reference, follow the cross-reference and run this step
   again in the borrowed chapter; the borrowing statute rarely defines what it borrowed.
3. **Structure and related statutes.** Surrounding subsections, the section's place in the
   chapter, closely-related statutes, cross-references in and out, and any **textually manifest
   purpose** stated in the statute itself. Quote what bears.
4. **Statutory history** — how the text itself has changed by amendment. Intrinsic, and not to be
   confused with legislative history, which is extrinsic and comes at step 10.
5. **Construction statutes.** § 990.001 and any enacted chapter-specific construction directive,
   including a liberal-construction directive within its scope — see § 3.2. Read the directive
   with the substantive provision and controlling decisions; its label does not decide its effect.
   **Read § 990.001's introductory clause first**: every rule in the chapter yields where
   applying it "would produce a result inconsistent with the manifest intent of the legislature."
   Quoting a subsection of § 990.001 without that qualifier overstates it.
6. **Intrinsic (textual) canons.** Whole-text, surplusage, *noscitur*, *ejusdem generis*,
   *expressio unius*, the last-antecedent and series-qualifier rules, absurdity avoidance. These
   are tools for reading the text and **need no ambiguity finding** — § 3.1. Each with a
   retrieved Wisconsin case and its opposing canon addressed.
7. **Binding construction of the same words.** A published Wisconsin appellate decision
   construing this text is not "case law you turn to if the text runs out"; it is part of what
   the text means. Run it through `authority-hierarchy.md` for what binds.

### Stage B — The plain-meaning call

8. **Plain meaning?** State the answer in a sentence, **on the whole of Stage A**. If the meaning
   is plain, the interpretive inquiry ends and you move to application — *Kalal* ¶ 45, as
   clarified by *SEIU* ¶ 10. Say what made it plain; "the words are clear" is not a finding.
9. **If ambiguous:** name the two or more senses and who reasonably holds each — *Kalal* ¶ 47.
   The test is not that the parties disagree (§ 1.4).

### Stage C — Resolve remaining ambiguity; distinguish extrinsic confirmation

10. **Substantive canons.** Lenity (which needs *grievous* ambiguity), constitutional avoidance,
    and generic judge-made remedial or liberal-construction maxims, subject to governing Wisconsin
    authority — § 3.2. This category does **not** postpone an enacted construction directive:
    statutory text belongs in Stage A. Do not use a generic policy maxim to manufacture ambiguity.
    **Retroactivity
    is deliberately not on this list**: § 3.2 refuses to state a retroactivity rule, and the
    operative-date question is normally answered mechanically at § 2.4 without reaching any
    presumption at all. If it is not, retrieve Wisconsin authority before asserting anything.
11. **Extrinsic sources**, only now: legislative history, drafting records, Legislative Reference
    Bureau analyses, Legislative Council notes. Also usable **after** a plain reading purely to
    confirm it — *Kalal* ¶ 51 — but never to create the ambiguity that lets you reach them.

### Stage D — Close

12. **Application** to the facts, element by element, each element with its own quote.
13. **Confidence** and what would change it.

Steps skipped are named as skipped. A short answer is fine; a silent gap is not.
