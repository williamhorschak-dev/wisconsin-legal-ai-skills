# May a 2015 unpublished Wisconsin Court of Appeals opinion be cited for its persuasive value?

*Specimen. See the banner above: the source paths are notional, no citator was run, and the law
is illustrative. Re-run every check before relying on any of it.*

## Question Presented

Whether a Wisconsin Court of Appeals opinion issued in 2015, authored by a member of a
three-judge panel and designated unpublished, may be cited in a Wisconsin Court of Appeals brief for
its persuasive value, and what a party citing it must do.

## Short Answer

Yes, for persuasive value only, provided the opinion was authored by a member of a three-judge
panel or by a single judge under Wis. Stat. § 752.31(2). It is not binding precedent and the
court need not distinguish it. The citing party must file and serve a copy of the opinion with
the brief. **Confidence: MODERATE — the authorities below were not Shepardized or KeyCited, and
under `sources.md` § 4 that caps the stated confidence regardless of how clean the text reads.**

## Governing Text

### Wis. Stat. § 809.23(3)

> "An unpublished opinion may not be cited in any court of this state as precedent or authority,
> except to support a claim of claim preclusion, issue preclusion, or the law of the case, and
> except as provided in par. (b)."

§ 809.23(3)(a).

> "In addition to the purposes specified in par. (a), an unpublished opinion issued on or after
> July 1, 2009, that is authored by a member of a three-judge panel or by a single judge under
> s. 752.31 (2) may be cited for its persuasive value. A per curiam opinion, memorandum opinion,
> summary disposition order, or other order is not an authored opinion for purposes of this
> subsection. Because an unpublished opinion cited for its persuasive value is not precedent,
> it is not binding on any court of this state. A court need not distinguish or otherwise
> discuss an unpublished opinion and a party has no duty to research or cite it."

§ 809.23(3)(b).

> "A party citing an unpublished opinion shall file and serve a copy of the opinion with the
> brief or other paper in which the opinion is cited."

§ 809.23(3)(c).

## Lens 1 — What the Law Is

**Stage A — intrinsic sources**

1. **Text.** Paragraph (a) states a prohibition with two carve-outs: three preclusion-family
   uses, and whatever par. (b) permits. Paragraph (b) is therefore an exception to a
   prohibition, and is read as one.
2. **Definitions.** "Unpublished opinion" and "three-judge panel" are not defined in § 809.23;
   ch. 809's structure and § 752.31 supply the operational meaning. § 990.01 adds nothing here.
3. **Structure and manifest purpose.** § 809.23(1) governs the publication criteria and
   § 809.23(3) governs citation of what is not published. The subsections work together: (3)
   presupposes (1)'s designation. The rule's evident object is to keep unpublished dispositions
   out of the precedent stream while allowing a narrow, disclosed persuasive use.
4. **Statutory history.** Par. (b)'s July 1, 2009 date is itself a history marker: the
   persuasive-value exception was added, and its date condition tracks that addition. `[FILL-IN]`
   a real memo pulls the amending Supreme Court Order and quotes what changed; this specimen
   does not.
5. **Construction statutes.** § 990.001 supplies nothing that changes the reading — and its
   introductory clause, which conditions every rule in the chapter on consistency with manifest
   legislative intent, is not triggered. Note § 990.001(6): a section title is not part of the
   statute. That is **not** a categorical bar on looking at a title (see `canons.md` § 2.2); it
   means a title cannot be the ground of the reading, and here nothing turns on one.
6. **Intrinsic canons — applied, not deferred.** These need no ambiguity finding, so they run
   here rather than waiting on step 8. Whole-text: par. (b)'s date, authorship conditions, and
   express exclusions limit its exception to par. (a). Per curiam opinions and the other listed
   dispositions are excluded by the second sentence; no inference from *expressio unius* is
   needed. In this appellate brief, (c)'s copy duty attaches to "a party citing an unpublished
   opinion," whether the permitted use rests on (a) or (b).
7. **Binding construction of the same words.** None found construing par. (b)'s authorship
   condition. That absence is itself the finding, and it is why the answer below turns on the
   face of the opinion rather than on a case.

**Stage B — the plain-meaning call**

8. **Plain meaning?** Plain, **on the whole of Stage A** rather than on par. (b) read alone.
   Paragraph (b) grants persuasive-value citation on two stated conditions — a date and an
   authorship requirement — and paragraph (c) imposes a filing duty on any party citing any
   unpublished opinion. Under *Kalal* ¶ 45, "[i]f the meaning of the statute is plain, we
   ordinarily stop the inquiry," and the inquiry stops here.
9. **If ambiguous.** Not reached.

**Stage C — not reached**

10. **Substantive canons.** Not reached; no ambiguity finding, and none of them is a reading tool.
11. **Extrinsic sources.** Not consulted; unnecessary under *Kalal* ¶ 46 ("Where statutory
    language is unambiguous, there is no need to consult extrinsic sources of interpretation,
    such as legislative history.").

A 2015 opinion satisfies the date condition. Whether it satisfies the authorship condition is a
question of fact about the specific opinion, answered from its face.

## Lens 2 — How Each Side Argues It

### Our best reading

The date and authorship conditions in par. (b) are both satisfied on the face of the opinion, so
the citation is permitted. Nothing in the text conditions persuasive-value citation on the
subject matter, the panel's reasoning, or the opponent's consent. Compliance with par. (c) is
mechanical: file and serve the copy.

### Their best reading

An opponent's strongest move is not to argue the citation is improper — the text does not support
that — but to argue it is worthless. Paragraph (b) itself says such an opinion is not binding
precedent and that the court need not distinguish it, so the opponent will say the court may
disregard it without explanation, which is exactly what the statute permits. A secondary move:
if the opinion is a per curiam, memorandum opinion, summary disposition order, or other order,
par. (b) expressly excludes it from persuasive-value use. Paragraph (a)'s preclusion and
law-of-the-case uses remain. Read the opinion's designation to determine which category applies.
